AEMO STEM curve forecasting

7-day-ahead forecasts of the demand, supply and residual curves of the Western Australian Short Term Energy Market, scored on the fixed test year from 1 May 2024 to 24 April 2025.

Data

The raw data are the bids and offers of the Short Term Energy Market (STEM) of Western Australia, the market run by the Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO). Each row of the monthly source files is one bid or one offer, with a price in $/MWh and a quantity in MWh. Every trading day holds 48 half-hour auctions, and one auction is named by its trading date and its interval number from 1 to 48. We call the rows marked as offers the supply side and the rows marked as bids the demand side.

For each auction we build three step curves of price against cumulative volume. The demand curve sorts the bids from the highest price down and adds up their quantities, so its price falls as volume grows. The supply curve sorts the offers from the lowest price up, so its price rises as volume grows. The residual curve is the demand price minus the supply price at each volume, so it also falls and it turns negative once supply becomes more expensive than demand. The volume where the residual curve reaches $0 is the market-clearing volume, the amount of energy the auction trades.

We fit on 1 January 2021 to 30 April 2024, which is 1,216 days and 58,368 auctions per side. We score on 1 May 2024 to 24 April 2025, which is 359 days and 17,232 auctions per side. The forecast horizon is 7 days, so the newest curve a model may read for a delivery day is the curve of 7 days earlier. A model that refits during the test year fits each test month on the data dated on or before the first day of that month minus 7 days. That data includes the earlier test months. The test window is fixed, and the leaderboard ranks every experiment on it.

Two ways of turning a curve into 64 numbers are used below. The interp embedding reads the price at 64 shares of the curve's own total volume, evenly spaced from 2% to 98%. The v-grid embedding reads the price at 64 volumes that are evenly spaced from 0 to a fixed volume cap, so the same position always means the same volume in every auction.

The score is HC-MAE. It is the mean absolute price difference between the predicted curve and the true curve, read on a common volume grid, in $/MWh, and lower is better. The Results section gives the full definition.

Exogenous features are the extra daily numbers a model may add to the curve numbers and the calendar features. Two sources of them are used: daily summaries of the market itself and daily weather values for Perth.

The rows marked Baseline in the results tables are learning-free models, so nothing is fitted. The lag-7 baseline (baseline.lag7) returns the curve of the same interval 7 days earlier without any change, and every Δ and every Win % below is measured against it. The same-weekday average (weekly.same_weekday_avg) takes the mean of the interp embeddings of the same interval on the four most recent same weekdays, which are 7, 14, 21 and 28 days earlier. Its curve carries the averaged prices, and its total volume is the average of the four total volumes.

The weighted median (weekly.wmedian) builds a new step curve from the curves of the same interval 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 21 and 28 days earlier. At each volume, its price is the weighted median of the prices of these 10 curves at that volume. The lag-7 curve has weight 2 and every other curve has weight 1, so the total weight is 11 and the price at each volume is one of the 10 prices. A curve's start volume is the end of its first step, and its end volume is the end of its last step. For demand and residual, the forecast starts at the median of the 10 start volumes and ends at the median of the 10 end volumes. For supply, it starts and ends where the lag-7 curve does.

The capped weighted median (weekly.wmedian_cap, supply only) is weekly.wmedian with one change at the price cap. The cap of a day is the highest offer price over all supply curves of that day, and the cap known for the delivery day D is the cap of day D-7. The change applies to each of the 10 curves whose own day has a cap different from the known cap, before the median. In such a curve, every step priced at or above the cap of its own day minus 0.5 $/MWh is set to the known cap. When the known cap is lower, every price above the known cap is also lowered to the known cap.

Method 1: Retrieval

This family works on the v-grid embedding, the 64 prices read at 64 volumes evenly spaced from 0 to a fixed volume cap. The cap is a high percentile of the total volume of the training auctions, so the few auctions with the largest volume do not raise it. The percentile is 99 for demand and residual, and 99.9 for supply. The Euclidean distance between two such vectors of 64 prices follows HC-MAE closely, so two curves that are close in the embedding are also close under the score.

The forecast works in two steps. First, for each of the 48 intervals we fit one ridge regression. It predicts the 64 numbers of the delivery day D from the 64 numbers of the same interval 7 days earlier, plus five calendar features. The calendar features are the day of week as a sine and a cosine, a weekend flag, and the month as a sine and a cosine. The exogenous features are added when the experiment uses them. The features are standardised and reduced to 20 principal components before they are given to the ridge, and the ridge penalty is 200. In the split variant (vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_split) the 20 components are taken from the 64 embedding numbers alone, and the calendar and exogenous features are standardised and passed on without compression.

Second, the predicted 64 numbers are used as a query. In every vgrid forecast except the two median variants below, the candidate set holds the real past curves of the same interval and the same weekday, and we return the one whose embedding is closest to the query in Euclidean distance. The forecast is then the step curve of that past auction exactly as the market produced it, and not an average or a smoothed shape. This is why we call the family retrieval, because the model searches the past for the curves closest to the query.

The rolling models refit at the start of every test month. The cut-off is the first day of that month minus 7 days, and the ridge uses only auctions dated on or before that cut-off. The candidate set uses the same auctions, except in the two median variants below. The training window expands, so a later month is fitted on more data than an earlier one. The plain rolling model (vgrid.rolling_ridge) adds no exogenous features. The fit-once model (vgrid.strat_knn) fits the ridge and the candidate set on the training window and never refits.

Three exogenous sources can be added to the features. The market-state proxy is built from the market itself. It holds thirteen daily numbers, all read from the day 7 days before the delivery day. Five describe that day's prices and volume, which are the mean, median and spread of the prices, the total volume, and the number of bid or offer rows. Two are read from that day's interp embeddings, which are the mean length of the 64-number vectors and their mean price range, both averaged over the 48 intervals. The other six are trend numbers, which are the 7-day and 28-day trailing mean price, two price trends, a price z-score and a volume trend. The proxy also adds the day of year as a sine and a cosine, and Western Australian holiday flags.

The weather source is ten daily Perth values, also read from 7 days before the delivery day. They are the highest, lowest and mean temperature, apparent temperature, rain, highest and mean wind speed, solar radiation, humidity and surface pressure. The weather_fc source is the same ten values measured on the delivery day itself, with random noise added per variable. The noise size per variable comes from published ECMWF and WeatherReal day-7 verification numbers. So weather_fc is the measured weather of the delivery day with random noise added, and not a real weather forecast.

The variants differ in which sources the ridge gets. vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_proxyonly uses the proxy alone. vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog and vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_split use the proxy and the weather source. vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_wfc uses the proxy and weather_fc. Each scenario runs the variants that its table lists.

The autoregressive variant adds the days inside the 7-day gap. The base model uses only the curve of 7 days earlier. The curves of the days D-1, D-2 and D-3 are closer to the delivery day, but they have not happened yet at forecast time. The variant fills them with the model's own predictions, taken from a walk-forward backfill. In that backfill the base model is refitted month by month, and each month is predicted from data at least 7 days older. Every backfilled prediction is therefore one the model could have made at the time. Each of the three predicted embeddings is reduced to 8 block means, and one flag per lag marks the rows where a real older curve was used because the backfill had no prediction. The block adds 3 x (8 + 1) = 27 numbers to the features. The autoregressive runs are vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_split_ar and vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_wfc_ar.

The two median variants (vgrid.recent_median and vgrid.recent_median_wfc, demand only) build a new step curve from several real curves. The query of vgrid.recent_median comes from a ridge set up as in vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_proxyonly, and the query of vgrid.recent_median_wfc comes from a ridge set up as in vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_wfc. Their candidate set holds the curves of the same interval on any weekday, dated from 34 to 7 days before the delivery day D. These 28 days are the newest days known at forecast time, so the candidate set moves with D and can hold auctions newer than the monthly cut-off.

The 5 candidates closest to the query are taken together with the curves of the same interval 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 21 and 28 days earlier, which gives 15 curves. At each volume, the forecast price is the weighted median of the prices of these 15 curves at that volume. Each of the 5 candidates has weight 1, and the lag-7 curve has weight 3. The curves 21 and 28 days earlier have weight 1 in vgrid.recent_median and weight 2 in vgrid.recent_median_wfc, and the other lagged curves have weight 1. The total weight is odd (17 in vgrid.recent_median and 19 in vgrid.recent_median_wfc), so the price at each volume is one of the 15 prices. The forecast starts at the median of the 15 start volumes and ends at the median of the 15 end volumes.

The oracle of this family (vgrid.rolling_oracle) reads the true curve. Out of the rolling candidate set, it returns the curve whose v-grid embedding is closest to the true curve's v-grid embedding in Euclidean distance, so for a test month it may also return a curve from an earlier test month. It uses the true curve, so it is not a forecast, and it is the ceiling of the rolling models that return one curve of that candidate set.

Method 2: Medoids

This family describes a curve by its distances to a fixed set of medoids, which are real training curves chosen by k-medoids. Two medoid sets are used. The first one uses Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) as the distance, and the second one uses HC-MAE.

DTW measures how different two step curves are. It matches the points of one curve to the points of the other, and it may match one point to several. Two curves with a different number of steps and a different total volume can therefore be compared. Each step is treated as a point with two coordinates, the price and the volume. The volume is first divided by the curve total and then multiplied by 50, so both coordinates count about the same. Matching is restricted to points that are at most 10 positions apart, which is called the Sakoe-Chiba band. The DTW dissimilarity is the square root of the summed squared distances between the matched points.

We take a subsample of 480 training curves, spread evenly over the 48 intervals, and compute the DTW dissimilarity between every pair. On that matrix we run k-medoids with the Partition Around Medoids algorithm. The algorithm picks K of the curves as medoids, and it then swaps a medoid for a non-medoid whenever that lowers the total distance from every curve to its nearest medoid. The K medoids are real curves and not averages. K is set per scenario, at 24 for demand, 20 for supply and 24 for residual.

Any curve is then described by K numbers, its DTW dissimilarity to each of the K medoids. A prediction of a curve becomes a prediction of those K numbers, and the forecast is the medoid with the smallest predicted number.

The predicted variant (medoids.dtw_predicted) fits one ridge regression per interval, with penalty 5.0. It predicts the K numbers of the delivery day from the K numbers of the same interval 7, 8, 14 and 21 days earlier, plus the five calendar features. The smallest of those lags is 7 days, so every number the ridge reads is available at forecast time. The medoid with the smallest predicted dissimilarity is the forecast curve.

The second medoid set holds 48 medoids, chosen under HC-MAE from the training curves only. The search reads each curve at 2,048 equally spaced volumes, which gives a fast approximation of HC-MAE. It runs FasterPAM, a fast version of Partition Around Medoids, on a sample of 12,000 training curves spread evenly over the months and intervals of the training window. Then, on all 58,368 training curves, each curve is assigned to its nearest medoid, and a medoid moves to another curve of its group when that lowers the summed distance from the medoid to the curves of the group. This step is repeated for at most 6 rounds. Any curve is then described by 48 numbers, its exact HC-MAE to each of the 48 medoids (the feature table hcmae_medoids_k48).

The HC-MAE oracle (medoids.hcmae_oracle) returns, for each auction, the medoid with the smallest HC-MAE to the true curve. Its score is the best that any method returning one of these 48 medoids can reach. It uses the true curve, so it is not a forecast.

The medoid-embedding forecast (medoids.hcmae_embedding, demand and supply) uses the 48 numbers in place of the 64 numbers of the v-grid embedding. For each interval, a ridge predicts the 48 numbers of the delivery day D from the 48 numbers of the same interval 7 days earlier, the five calendar features and the market-state proxy. The ridge is set up as in vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_proxyonly, with 20 principal components, penalty 200 and a refit at the start of every test month. The candidate set holds the curves of the same interval on any weekday, dated from 34 to 7 days before D. The candidates whose 48 numbers are closest to the prediction in Euclidean distance are kept, 5 of them for demand and 10 for supply. At each volume, the forecast price is the median of their prices at that volume, so the forecast is a new step curve and not a medoid. It starts at the median of their start volumes and ends at the median of their end volumes.

Method 3: Adaptive

The adaptive family decides, for each auction, how much weight to give the lag-7 baseline and how much to give the retrieval prediction. The two candidates are the lag-7 baseline curve (baseline.lag7) and the curve returned by vgrid.rolling_ridge_exog_split. A weight w between 0 and 1 sets the mix: w = 1 keeps the baseline curve, w = 0 keeps the retrieval curve.

The two step curves are what gets mixed. For a weight strictly between 0 and 1 we take the union of the volume break-points of both curves. We read the price of each curve at every point of that union, and we average the two prices with weights w and 1 - w. At w = 1 and w = 0 the original curve is returned unchanged, so a blend at an end point scores exactly what that curve scores on its own.

The weighting rule is refitted once per test month, and every feature and every fitted model for a month uses only rows dated at least 7 days before that month starts. adaptive.constant_blend and adaptive.stratum_blend then apply one weight to the whole month, one weight per stratum in the second case. The other five rules give every auction its own weight.

Two rules read one more thing. adaptive.prob_blend fits its isotonic correction, and adaptive.regret_blend picks its temperature, on the realised errors of the earlier test months. Those months include the six days that sit between the cut-off and the first day of the month being predicted.

  • adaptive.constant_blend uses one weight for the whole month. The weight is picked from the grid 0 to 1 in steps of 0.05. It is the value that makes the blended curves of the earlier rows as close to the truth as possible. When there are more than 3,000 earlier rows, the grid is scored on a fixed random subsample of 3,000 of them.
  • adaptive.stratum_blend uses one weight per stratum, where a stratum is a block of four neighbouring intervals crossed with weekday against weekend. Each stratum takes the best grid weight on its own earlier rows, shrunk toward the single global weight by how many rows it has. A stratum with fewer than 50 rows uses the global weight, and a stratum with more than 3,000 rows is scored on a fixed random subsample of 3,000 of them.
  • adaptive.prob_blend fits a gradient-boosted classifier that predicts the chance that the baseline curve has a smaller error than the retrieval curve. Its features are the interval number and its four-interval block, the two retrieval distances, the size of the candidate set, and the distance between the predicted embedding and the lag-7 embedding. It also uses the recent realised errors of both candidates per interval and per interval and weekday, how far the lag-7 curve is from the lag-14 and lag-28 curves, and calendar flags. The predicted chance is then corrected by an isotonic regression, a rule fitted on the earlier test months that never turns a larger predicted chance into a smaller weight. The corrected value is the weight.
  • adaptive.prob_blend_raw takes the predicted chance of the same classifier as the weight, with no correction.
  • adaptive.regret_blend fits a gradient-boosted regression on the error gap, which is the retrieval error minus the baseline error, and turns the predicted gap into a weight with a sigmoid. The baseline gets more weight where the retrieval curve is predicted to be worse. The gap is divided by a temperature before the sigmoid, so a larger temperature makes the weight move more slowly with the gap. The temperature is picked each month from the grid 5, 15, 40 and 100 on the earlier test months, and the first test month uses the grid median because it has no earlier month.
  • adaptive.oracle_weight reads the true curve and picks, for each auction, the grid weight with the smallest realised error, so it can also mix the two. It is the ceiling of the whole family and not a forecast.

A separate blend family mixes the curve of the same interval at several weekly lags instead of mixing the baseline with a retrieval prediction. It works on the interp embeddings, so the 64 numbers of each lagged curve are averaged with weights, and the averaged prices are then put back on a step curve. blend.blended_lag learns one weight per interval and weekday over the lag-7 and lag-14 curves. blend.learned_blend learns weights over the lag-7, lag-14, lag-21 and lag-28 curves that are not negative and add up to one. blend.ema_baseline uses the fixed weights 0.6, 0.2 and 0.2 over the lag-7, lag-14 and lag-21 curves. These three rows carry the Adaptive label in the results tables.

Results

Every forecast below is 7 days ahead, and there is one table per scenario: Demand, Supply and Residual. A table lists the experiments of that scenario, ranked by the score. The Method column names the family the experiment belongs to, which is Baseline, Retrieval, Medoids, Adaptive, Diagnostic or Other.

Diagnostic holds two rows that move the whole lag-7 curve up or down by one price, to measure how much a shift alone can do. diagnostic.realized_shift adds 0.3 times the median price difference between the lag-7 curve and the lag-14 curve. diagnostic.oracle_shift adds the median price difference between the true curve and the lag-7 curve, both read on the 64 embedding positions. Other holds the post-hoc correctors (corrector), the functional-data models (fda), the distance and metric-learning variants (distance, metric_learn) and the probabilistic variants (probabilistic).

The volume-shift corrector (corrector.volume_shift_wfc, demand only) starts from the pointwise median of the 14 curves of the same interval 7 to 20 days earlier. This median curve starts at the smallest of the 14 start volumes and ends at the median of the 14 end volumes. The cliff volume of a demand curve is the volume of the bids priced above the -1,000 $/MWh price floor. A gradient-boosted tree model with the absolute error loss forecasts the cliff volume of each auction. It is refitted at the start of every test month on the rows dated on or before the first day of that month minus 7 days. The median curve then moves along the volume axis by the forecast cliff volume minus its own cliff volume, and its start and end volumes do not change. The model reads the cliff volumes and the end volumes of the curves 7 to 34 days earlier, the daily mean cliff volumes of the same days, the calendar of the delivery day, the market-state proxy and weather_fc. weather_fc is a simulated forecast, the actual Perth weather of the delivery day plus random noise, and not a real weather forecast.

The mark "(oracle)" after the family name means the row reads the true curve, so it is not available at forecast time. Four families show one oracle row each: vgrid.rolling_oracle, medoids.hcmae_oracle, adaptive.oracle_weight and diagnostic.oracle_shift (residual has no Diagnostic rows). Each of them is described above with its family.

The row colours follow the score. The lag-7 baseline row is grey. Rows that score better than the baseline are green, and the green is stronger for a lower HC-MAE. Rows that score worse than the baseline go from yellow to orange to red as HC-MAE rises. Oracle rows are blue.

HC-MAE is the score. It reads the predicted curve and the true curve at 10,000 equally spaced volumes that span both curves, and it averages the absolute price difference. The unit is $/MWh and lower is better. Δ vs lag-7 is the score minus the score of the lag-7 baseline on the same auctions, so a negative value means the model is better than the baseline. The 95% CI is a confidence interval for that difference from a cluster bootstrap that resamples whole trading days (2,000 resamples, seed 42). A "*" after the interval means the whole interval is below 0 and a Holm-corrected one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives p < 0.05. The correction covers all the non-baseline rows of the table. Win % is the share of the 17,232 auctions where the model's error is smaller than the baseline's error.

The last two columns measure the error in market terms. We cross the predicted curve with the true curve of the other side (the residual curve is crossed with the $0 line) to get a predicted clearing volume V*. We then read the clearing price P* off the true supply curve at that volume. |ΔV*| in MWh and |ΔP*| in $/MWh are how far the prediction moves the clearing point away from the true one. A few price-floor auctions make the mean of |ΔP*| large, so the median is the summary. These two columns are reported next to the score and never used for ranking.
